# FTC opens inquiry into Big Tech's partnerships with leading AI startups

## Factual Summary

- On January 25, 2024, AP reported that the Federal Trade Commission opened a compulsory-order inquiry into major AI-related investments and partnerships involving large technology firms and leading AI startups.
- The report said the FTC sought information from Alphabet, Amazon, Anthropic, Microsoft, and OpenAI about the structure and competitive consequences of these relationships.
- AP framed the inquiry as part of a broader official effort to understand whether AI concentration was being reinforced through capital ties, cloud dependence, and privileged market access.

## Key Quotes or Findings

- AP reported that the FTC was using its 6(b) authority to demand records about the partnerships.
- The article connected the inquiry to concerns about competition, market dominance, and the future structure of the AI industry.
- The piece treated startup-hyperscaler ties as a live antitrust and public-policy issue, not just a business-development story.

## What It Proves

- That formal competition scrutiny of frontier-AI partnerships was already underway in the United States by January 2024.
- That Chapter 10 can ground its competition-policy response layer in documented concerns about structural dependence between model labs and major platforms.
- That a response agenda focused on concentration, interoperability, and structural remedies is anchored in real regulatory attention rather than in abstract anti-corporate rhetoric.

## What It Suggests

- That the relevant policy unit is not only the model company by itself, but also the cloud, capital, and distribution relationships that shape market power.
- That response to AI concentration should include inquiry into dependency-producing partnership structures, not only after-the-fact product regulation.

## What It Does Not Prove

- It does not prove that any of the investigated firms violated antitrust law.
- It does not prove that one inquiry by itself would produce structural remedies.
- It does not prove that U.S. regulators had already resolved how to balance competition concerns against national-security or industrial-policy arguments.
