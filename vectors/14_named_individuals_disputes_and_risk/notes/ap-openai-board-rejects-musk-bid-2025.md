# OpenAI board unanimously rejects Elon Musk's $97.4 billion proposal

## Factual Summary

- AP reported on February 14, 2025 that OpenAI's board unanimously rejected Elon Musk's $97.4 billion proposal to acquire the nonprofit entity that controls OpenAI.
- The same report records both Musk's allegation that OpenAI betrayed its nonprofit founding aims and OpenAI's response that the proposal was not in the best interests of its mission.
- This source clears the threshold for vector 14 because it is a high-credibility dispute directly tied to Chapter 8's structural question: who gets to control the mission, assets, and governance shell of a frontier AI institution.

## Key Quotes or Findings

- AP says Bret Taylor stated that OpenAI was not for sale and that the board unanimously rejected Musk's latest attempt to disrupt his competition.
- OpenAI attorney William Savitt said the proposal was not in the best interests of OpenAI's mission and was rejected.
- AP reports that Musk sued over what he said was a betrayal of OpenAI's founding nonprofit aims.
- The article also says a judge was still considering Musk's request and had expressed skepticism about some of his claims.

## What It Proves

- That there was an active, public, high-stakes dispute over control of OpenAI's nonprofit mission structure.
- That Musk's allegation was procedurally live but contested, and that OpenAI publicly rejected both the bid and Musk's framing.
- That named-individual material can enter the dossier when it illuminates institutional control rather than simply adding scandal texture.

## What It Suggests

- That the governance shell around frontier AI firms has become valuable enough to trigger direct conflict over mission, assets, and conversion pathways.
- That the nonprofit-versus-for-profit question is not merely symbolic; it shapes who can legitimately claim stewardship over the institution.

## What It Does Not Prove

- It does not prove Musk's claims are correct.
- It does not prove OpenAI's response is complete or neutral.
- It does not justify guilt by association against any person named in the dispute; the relevance here is structural, not moral contamination.
