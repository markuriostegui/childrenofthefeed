# Social Media Seen as Mostly Good for Democracy Across Many Nations, But U.S. is a Major Outlier

## Factual Summary

- Pew Research Center reports substantial cross-national differences in how people judge social media's impact on democracy, but it identifies the United States as a clear negative outlier.
- The report says only 34% of U.S. adults surveyed viewed social media as good for democracy, while 64% said it had a bad impact.
- Across the 19 countries surveyed, a median of 84% said internet and social media access had made people easier to manipulate with false information and rumors.
- A median of 65% said these systems had made people more divided in their political opinions, and more than four in ten said they had made political discussion less civil.

## Key Quotes or Findings

- Pew says the United States is a “clear outlier” on this question, with much stronger negative assessments than the other countries surveyed.
- The report records a median of 84% across 19 countries saying the internet and social media have made people easier to manipulate with false information and rumors.
- It also reports a median of 65% saying these systems have made people more divided in their political opinions.
- In the U.S. specifically, 79% said social media had made people more divided and 69% said it had made them less civil in how they talk about politics.

## What It Proves

- That cross-national evidence supports the claim that social media is widely associated with manipulation, division, and declining civility, even where some respondents still see democratic benefits.
- That the United States can be treated as an unusually severe case in the trust-and-fragmentation story rather than as just another generic example.
- That Chapter 2 can connect platform-mediated social erosion to democratic trust and civic coherence with stronger evidence than youth-harm studies alone provide.

## What It Suggests

- That platform harms should be read not only through mental-health or attention metrics, but also through their effects on civic legitimacy and shared interpretive ground.
- That the same systems can feel empowering and corrosive at once, which helps explain why platform dependence persists even when trust deteriorates.
- That the dossier can discuss institutional and moral erosion without collapsing into a one-dimensional anti-technology story.

## What It Does Not Prove

- It does not prove that social media alone caused democratic decline or institutional distrust in any country.
- It does not establish a clean causal chain from platform use to every later form of political polarization.
- It does not prove the dossier's broader moral interpretation without support from other sources on youth harm, monetization, and cultural incentives.
