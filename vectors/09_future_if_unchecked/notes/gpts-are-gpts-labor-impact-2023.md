# GPTs are GPTs: An Early Look at the Labor Market Impact Potential of Large Language Models

## Factual Summary

- The March 17, 2023 paper evaluates occupational exposure to large language models by mapping model capabilities to work tasks in the U.S. labor market.
- The authors report broad task exposure, with higher exposure concentrated in many higher-wage and knowledge-intensive occupations rather than only in low-skill routine work.
- The paper distinguishes task exposure from full job replacement and explicitly treats model-assisted work as a channel for both productivity gains and restructuring pressure.

## Key Quotes or Findings

- The paper argues that LLMs have economy-wide relevance because many occupations contain language-based tasks that models can partially perform or accelerate.
- It reports that exposure is not evenly distributed and is often higher in occupations with more formal education and higher wages.
- It treats task-level impact as the correct unit of analysis, which matters for later arguments about deskilling, reorganization, and bargaining power.

## What It Proves

- That generative AI exposure reaches well beyond narrow routine clerical work and extends into professional and white-collar task bundles.
- That a future scenario of labor stratification can be grounded in documented differential exposure rather than in pure speculation.
- That Chapter 09 can discuss weakening bargaining power for parts of the technical and professional middle class without claiming universal job extinction.

## What It Suggests

- That continuity under current deployment patterns is likely to produce uneven restructuring rather than a single clean wave of total automation.
- That later chapters on layoffs, bargaining, and access hierarchy can be connected back to a documented task-exposure base.

## What It Does Not Prove

- It does not prove that exposed occupations will all lose jobs in equal measure.
- It does not prove that productivity gains will be distributed fairly across workers, firms, and sectors.
- It does not prove that labor-market outcomes are technologically fixed rather than mediated by institutions and policy.
