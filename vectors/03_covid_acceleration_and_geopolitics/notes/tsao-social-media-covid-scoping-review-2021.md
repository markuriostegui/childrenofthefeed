# What social media told about us in the time of COVID-19: a scoping review

## Factual Summary

This review examines 81 empirical studies on COVID-19 and social media covering the first outbreak period from November 2019 through May 2020. It argues that social media rapidly became a central infrastructure for information generation, dissemination, and consumption during the pandemic, while also serving as a major channel for misinformation, mental-health discourse, public-attitude tracking, and analysis of government response.

## Key Quotes or Findings

- The abstract states that social media "rapidly become a crucial communication tool for information generation, dissemination, and consumption."
- The review identifies major themes including public attitudes, infodemics, mental health, case detection or prediction, government response, and the quality of health-information videos.
- The authors also note a lack of real-time surveillance studies built with social-media data, which matters because it shows both the promise and the incompleteness of early pandemic platform monitoring.

## What It Proves

- That pandemic dependence on digital platforms was not limited to remote work.
- That social media became a critical layer of mediation for health information, public interpretation, mental-health discourse, and misinformation management during the first COVID wave.
- That Chapter 3 can support claims about platform-mediated pandemic life with evidence broader than workplace digitization alone.

## What It Suggests

For Chapter 3, this supports a broader argument that COVID accelerated everyday dependence on platform-mediated reality, not only labor digitization. It also helps connect the pandemic to intensified informational vulnerability, where attention, trust, and public health interpretation became more dependent on privately governed feeds and recommendation systems.

## What It Does Not Prove

This review does not prove that social media caused all pandemic-era mental-health or political harms, nor does it prove that governments or platforms acted with unified strategic intent. It should be used to support the scale of mediation and dependence, not monocausal claims.
