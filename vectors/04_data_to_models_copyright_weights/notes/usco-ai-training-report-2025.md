# Copyright and Artificial Intelligence, Part 3: Generative AI Training (Pre-publication Version)

## Factual Summary

- This May 2025 U.S. Copyright Office report addresses whether and how copyrighted works may be used to train generative AI systems.
- The report says generative AI systems draw on massive amounts of data, including copyrighted works, and frames the issue as a question of consent, compensation, fair use, and licensing feasibility.
- It explicitly notes that dozens of lawsuits were already pending in the United States and that the public-policy challenge is to balance technological innovation with a viable creative ecosystem.

## Key Quotes or Findings

- The report says the technologies involved draw on “massive troves of data, including copyrighted works.”
- It says the public interest requires “striking an effective balance” between innovation and maintaining a thriving creative community.
- It notes that the Office's August 2023 NOI asked technical questions about how copyrighted works are collected, curated, and used in training AI models, legal questions about fair use, and factual questions about licensing.
- The table of contents shows sustained treatment of prima facie infringement, fair use, market harm, and licensing options.

## What It Proves

- That the U.S. Copyright Office treats AI training on copyrighted works as a major live legal and policy problem rather than a settled matter.
- That official analysis recognizes copyrighted creative works as central inputs to generative AI development.
- That Chapter 4 can ground its argument about training-data appropriation and licensing conflict in a primary U.S. government source.

## What It Suggests

- Review causal relationships carefully before elevating them into a thesis.
- That the enclosure struggle over training data and model capability is already institutionalized at the highest policy level.
- That the conflict over weights and training inputs is not only philosophical; it is being translated into concrete disputes over consent, compensation, and market substitution.

## What It Does Not Prove

- It does not itself decide the pending lawsuits.
- It does not prove that all AI training is unlawful or that all licensing models are feasible.
- It does not prove the broader political thesis of “technical empire” without help from other vectors and synthesis.
