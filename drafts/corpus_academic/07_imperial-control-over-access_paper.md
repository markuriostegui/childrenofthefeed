# Title

Imperial Control Over Access: Chokepoints, Clouds, and Protected Lanes

## Abstract

This paper argues that frontier AI is governed through chokepoints rather than through open market access alone. Export controls, cloud dependence, government-only offerings, classified distribution channels, and military-adjacent interfaces create a stratified access regime in which intelligence becomes a permissioned infrastructure.[1][2][3][4][5][6]

## Keywords

export controls; compute chokepoints; cloud concentration; procurement; national security; access control

## Research Question

What kinds of documented chokepoints govern access to frontier AI, and how do they change the politics of who gets to build or use advanced systems?

## Central Thesis

Frontier AI access is increasingly governed through policy, infrastructure, and accredited lanes rather than by open competition alone. That makes the access regime look imperial not because nothing diffuses, but because the most consequential pathways remain highly governable.[1][2][4][5]

## Evidence and Method Note

This paper combines official policy materials, corporate infrastructure disclosures, and primary public-sector offering records. It focuses on documented chokepoints and avoids claims of total closure or hidden command.[1][2][3][4]

## Main Analysis

Executive action, federal requests for information, and corporate submissions establish that AI leadership had already become a formal policy project by 2025.[1][2][3] BIS guidance then supplied an explicit enforcement layer for the advanced-computing export regime.[4]

Cloud concentration compounds the policy layer. Anthropic's public strategic alignment with AWS shows that frontier development already depends materially on a small number of infrastructural landlords.[5]

The public-sector channel is no longer indirect. OpenAI's government offering, Anthropic's government-only model line, and the Palantir-AWS-Anthropic classified stack show that privileged access is increasingly organized through accredited, restricted, and state-priority lanes.[6][7][8]

The Army's `Detachment 201` adds a personnel interface. Senior frontier-tech executives can now move into a public military innovation structure without the state-company boundary remaining fully external.[9]

## Counterarguments

This paper does not claim that all frontier knowledge is sealed or that publication has ended. DeepSeek-style diffusion still matters. The stronger claim is that chips, clouds, accreditation, procurement, and policy access remain highly concentrated even amid partial technical leakage.[5][8]

## Limits of the Evidence

The record is strongest on chokepoints, policy sequence, and formal state-company interfaces. It is weaker on deeper contract-level procurement execution or any theory of total unified control.

## Reform Relevance

This chapter is one of the core evidentiary bases for `Limit Government Capture by AI Companies`. The more public support, export-control protection, and privileged state access matter, the weaker the case for treating the resulting intelligence as a purely private good.[4][6][9]

## Conclusion

The most consequential frontier AI lanes are not simply sold. They are governed. That makes access itself part of the empire problem the dossier is trying to name.[1][4][6]
