# Title

The Three-Reform Program: Platform Accountability, Patrimony of Weights, and Anti-Capture Doctrine

## Abstract

This paper presents the dossier's final policy synthesis. It argues that the evidence assembled across the project justifies a three-part structural response: refactor Section 230 for sovereign-scale platforms, treat frontier AI weights as patrimony-oriented infrastructure, and limit government capture by AI companies through heightened public obligations.[1][2][3][4][5][6]

## Keywords

Section 230; patrimony; AI weights; government capture; public obligations; platform accountability

## Research Question

What reform program follows if the dossier's evidence about extraction, enclosure, chokepoints, and legitimacy is taken seriously as one political structure?

## Central Thesis

The appropriate response is not an open-ended ethics menu. It is a three-reform doctrine that reconnects platform accountability, public claims over collectively trained intelligence, and anti-capture limits on state-company AI entanglement.[1][2][3]

## Evidence and Method Note

This paper is openly normative, but it is built from upstream documentary chapters. Each reform is presented as a doctrine argued from evidence rather than as a false claim that the policy outcome is already settled law or consensus.[1][2][4]

## Main Analysis

### Refactor Section 230

The platform record shows that sovereign-scale systems do not merely host user speech. They structure recommendation, amplification, targeting, monetization, and moderation conditions at mass scale.[1][2] A reformed Section `230` should therefore track systemic reach and design responsibility rather than rely on the obsolete passive-intermediary fiction. Child-safety duties, recommendation auditability, exposure accountability, and sovereign-scale platform-risk obligations belong inside that framework.[1][2]

### AI Weights as Patrimony of Humanity

The training-data and weights record shows that frontier capability is built from collectively produced language, culture, code, and labor under legal conditions that remain contested.[3][4] The dossier's normative conclusion is that such weights should not be treated as ordinary private property by default. Public-trust logic, anti-monopoly limits, democratic oversight, and restrictions on exclusive commercialization are therefore legitimate directions to test. In its strongest form, this doctrine pushes toward AI treated as open and broadly accessible civic-utility infrastructure rather than permanently enclosed premium scarcity.[3][4]

### Limit Government Capture by AI Companies

The access and legitimacy record shows that frontier firms already benefit from policy participation, strategic state channels, export-control protection, public-sector offerings, lobbying systems, and military-adjacent interfaces.[5][6] Any firm receiving material public support, procurement access, national-security privilege, or protective policy treatment should therefore face heightened transparency, conflict-of-interest rules, procurement visibility, model-risk disclosures, independent audits, and limits on exclusive private benefit.[5][6]

## Counterarguments

The most common objection is that these three reforms point in different directions. The dossier argues the opposite: they are coherent because platform extraction, weights enclosure, and state-company capture are mutually reinforcing parts of the same structure.[1][3][5]

## Limits of the Evidence

This paper supports a clear reform doctrine, not a completed statutory blueprint. The evidence is already strong enough to justify direction and burden of proof, but not every implementation detail has been resolved at the same level.

## Reform Relevance

This paper is itself the project's reform chapter. Cognitive literacy, labor organization, competition policy, and public-interest digital infrastructure remain necessary, but they function here as implementation arms of the three doctrines rather than as separate alternatives.[2][6]

## Conclusion

If human civilization was used to train the machine, then no private actor should hold unconditional, opaque, and exclusive dominion over the machine. Any legitimate control must remain publicly bounded, democratically contestable, and subordinate to anti-enclosure rules.[3][5]
