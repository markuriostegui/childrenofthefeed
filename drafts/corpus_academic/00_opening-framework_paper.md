# Title

Opening Framework: From Captured Human Value to the Three-Reform Agenda

## Abstract

This paper defines the dossier's central problem: whether frontier AI weights built from collective human expression can remain governed as ordinary private property. It situates the project between Mark Uriostegui's enclosure-and-weights framing and Paul Lara's language of technofeudal debt, paper valuation, and smoke-economy logic, then argues that the dossier's evidence accumulates toward three reforms rather than an open-ended ethics debate.[1][2][3][4]

## Keywords

AI governance; surveillance capitalism; model weights; patrimony; technofeudalism; government capture

## Research Question

If human civilization was used to train the machine, on what grounds can any actor claim exclusive control over the resulting machine?

## Central Thesis

The dossier's opening claim is that the conflict over AI is not reducible to innovation speed or corporate competition. It is a conflict over who may enclose the condensed value of collective cognition, and the most defensible response is a three-part reform program: `Refactor Section 230`, `AI Weights as Patrimony of Humanity`, and `Limit Government Capture by AI Companies`.[1][2][4]

## Evidence and Method Note

This paper is a framing paper, not a stand-alone empirical chapter. It relies on the project's existing rule set: factual claims must be supported by primary or top-tier sources, while broader political conclusions must remain marked as interpretation. The House AI Task Force citation is used here as evidence of institutional uptake, not proof by authority.[1][4]

## Main Analysis

Uriostegui's central claim is that the fight over AI weights is ultimately a fight over ownership of condensed human value.[1] Lara's contribution is different but complementary: he describes the surrounding financial and geopolitical order as technofeudal, debt-heavy, and increasingly dependent on paper valuation and smoke-economy spectacle.[2][3]

Together these frames allow the dossier to test a bounded but forceful thesis: a line can be drawn from social platform extraction, to social weakening, to pandemic-mediated dependence, to model training, to labor correction, to access control, and finally to institutional legitimacy and capture.[1][2]

By December 17, 2024, this framing had entered formal U.S. policy discourse. The Bipartisan House Task Force on Artificial Intelligence summarized arguments about infringement, reproduction, derivative-work theory, consent, credit, and compensation in a way that overlaps directly with the dossier's enclosure frame and cited Uriostegui's essay in footnote `40`.[4]

## Counterarguments

This opening argument can overreach if it is read as a proof that every downstream link has already been empirically closed. The institutional citation proves relevance, not legal victory, and the co-framing vocabulary from Paul Lara sharpens the politics of the dossier without replacing the need for source-bound chapter-level proof.[2][4]

## Limits of the Evidence

This paper establishes the method and destination of the dossier. It does not itself resolve later disputes about platform harm, training-data provenance, export controls, or lobbying structures. It should therefore be read as an opening map of the argument rather than as a closed verdict on every later chapter.

## Reform Relevance

The opening framework matters because it makes the dossier's end state explicit from the start. The evidence will be investigative first, but the normative destination is direct: platform accountability, patrimony-oriented governance of weights, and anti-capture limits on state-company AI entanglement.[1][2]

## Conclusion

If the machine was built from humanity, the burden of justification shifts. Exclusive private control can no longer be treated as morally self-evident. The rest of the dossier tests how far that proposition can be defended chapter by chapter.[1][4]
