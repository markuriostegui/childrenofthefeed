# Title

Future if Unchecked: Dependency, Access Hierarchy, and Cognitive Rent

## Abstract

This paper develops the dossier's continuity scenario. Rather than predicting cinematic AI takeover, it argues that the most credible future under current conditions is one of deeper dependency, more hierarchical access to intelligence, weaker middle-class bargaining power, and reduced cognitive sovereignty.[1][2][3][4][5]

## Keywords

future of work; cognitive sovereignty; compute concentration; dependency; hierarchy; AI governance

## Research Question

What future becomes most plausible if present platform habits, weights enclosure, labor correction, and access chokepoints continue without structural reform?

## Central Thesis

The most credible continuity scenario is not total machine replacement of humanity. It is a regime of dependency, access hierarchy, and shrinking autonomy organized around concentrated infrastructure and unequal bargaining power.[1][2][3]

## Evidence and Method Note

This paper is explicitly scenario-based. It uses current institutional and research evidence to project bounded futures, and it states clearly where it moves from documented fact to interpretation.[1][2][3][4]

## Main Analysis

The education-governance record already treats AI-mediated learning as a problem of oversight, privacy, bias, and human judgment rather than a neutral productivity upgrade.[1]

Labor exposure is also uneven by design. The GPTs paper and the IMF note show that generative-AI exposure reaches deep into professional and white-collar task bundles while remaining highly uneven across occupation, income, and geography.[2][3]

Compute-governance research shows that access to top-tier capability is materially shaped by infrastructure concentration. High-end intelligence therefore risks becoming a stratified utility long before it becomes universally empowering.[4]

The BIS warning and Dario Amodei's concentration warning show that both financial fragility and social-contract strain already sit inside the present trajectory.[5][6]

## Counterarguments

This paper does not claim an inevitable collapse path. More egalitarian outcomes remain possible if institutions change course. The chapter argues for urgency, not for prophecy.[1][3][4]

## Limits of the Evidence

The record is stronger on labor exposure, governance concern, and infrastructure concentration than on long-run learning outcomes or a single deterministic future path.

## Reform Relevance

This paper is where the three reforms become jointly necessary. Platform immunity, private enclosure of weights, and opaque state-company entanglement reinforce one another under the continuity scenario, which is why the response cannot remain fragmented.[1][4][6]

## Conclusion

If no structural reform occurs, the likely result is not simply "more AI." It is a society increasingly organized around cognitive rent, managed dependence, and unequal access to machine-mediated intelligence.[2][4][5]
