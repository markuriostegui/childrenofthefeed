# Title

Social Networks as Infrastructure for Free Data Capture

## Abstract

This paper argues that social platforms created the behavioral raw material later exploited by the AI economy. Using FTC enforcement, platform filings, and Meta's own rebrand and reporting record, it shows that sovereign-scale platforms are best understood as extractive, design-heavy infrastructures rather than passive intermediaries.[1][2][3][4][5]

## Keywords

social media; surveillance capitalism; data extraction; Meta; Facebook; Section 230

## Research Question

How did social platforms normalize the large-scale capture of behavior, and what does that do to the legal fiction of passive intermediation?

## Central Thesis

Social platforms converted social life into free behavioral infrastructure for extraction and monetization. That documented record weakens the passive-intermediary frame and begins the dossier's case for refactoring Section 230 around reach, design, amplification, targeting, and systemic harm.[1][2]

## Evidence and Method Note

This paper is built primarily on `T1` regulatory and corporate documents, with bounded use of structural-adjacency material. It does not attempt to prove a full training-data pipeline from every platform to every frontier model; it focuses on extraction architecture and governance continuity.[1][2][3][5]

## Main Analysis

The FTC's 2024 social-media and video-streaming report documents intensive extraction, retention, combination, and monetization of user data, making it difficult to describe sovereign-scale platforms as neutral speech conduits.[1]

The FTC's 2019 Facebook action matters because the Cambridge Analytica episode was not merely a media scandal. It produced structural compliance obligations, board-level oversight duties, and a formal record of deceptive and insufficiently controlled data governance.[2]

Facebook's `S-1` and later Meta governance documents show that platform surveillance and elite governance continuity belonged to the same corporate story. Peter Thiel's role is relevant here as a documented governance fact, not as proof of hidden command.[3][5]

Meta's 2021 rebrand and later `10-K` reporting show that the company attached a metaverse-centered strategic narrative to a still-dominant advertising engine. The rebrand redirected the story, but not the extractive business core.[4][5]

## Counterarguments

This chapter should not be used to claim that every social-media dataset is already fully mapped into later AI training mixtures. The stronger and better-supported claim is narrower: large platforms established the social, technical, and economic logic of capture that later AI systems inherited.[1][4]

## Limits of the Evidence

The record is strongest on platform extraction, regulatory response, and governance continuity. It is weaker on any maximal intelligence-agency theory or total training-stack continuity claim and should remain bounded accordingly.[3][5]

## Reform Relevance

This paper supports `Refactor Section 230` by showing that sovereign-scale platforms operate through product design, recommendation logic, monetization architecture, and persistent exposure shaping. That is a different legal object from a passive host.[1][2]

## Conclusion

The AI empire did not begin with models. It began with platforms that normalized large-scale behavioral capture and built the legal and economic habits later systems would inherit.[1][2][4]
