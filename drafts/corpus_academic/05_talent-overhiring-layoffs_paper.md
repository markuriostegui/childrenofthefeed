# Title

Talent Hoarding, Valuation, Section 174, and the Politics of Layoffs

## Abstract

This paper argues that the big tech layoff cycle was not caused by AI alone. Using IRS guidance, congressional materials, executive statements, and macro-financial warnings, it shows that AI functioned as a legitimizing narrative layered on top of a broader structural correction involving overhiring, rates, margin pressure, and research-cost treatment.[1][2][3][4][5]

## Keywords

layoffs; Section 174; overhiring; AI hype; valuation; labor

## Research Question

How should the tech layoff cycle be understood if AI is treated as a narrative layer rather than as a total causal explanation?

## Central Thesis

The post-pandemic correction in tech labor was driven by multiple structural pressures. AI became the language through which that correction was narrated, justified, and politically normalized.[1][2][3]

## Evidence and Method Note

This paper prioritizes official tax and legislative material, direct company framing, and top-tier reporting. It avoids reducing layoffs to a single cause and instead reconstructs the broader correction that AI later helped explain.[1][2][4]

## Main Analysis

Section `174` matters because IRS guidance and subsequent congressional repair efforts show that domestic research and software costs were forced into a less favorable amortization structure after 2021. For research-heavy firms, this changed the financial environment materially.[1][2]

The overhiring record is also clear. Meta's own "year of efficiency" language and major reporting across Meta, Amazon, and Salesforce show that firms were already correcting managerial excess and cost structure after a period of aggressive pandemic expansion.[3][4][5]

Top-tier reporting links the broader malaise to weak demand, investor impatience, and fragile margins. The correction therefore existed before AI became the universal narrative used to explain or dignify it.[5][6]

The BIS annual report adds the wider macro-financial risk: the AI capex race was outrunning earnings and free cash flow, exposing the buildout to financing fragility rather than stable abundance.[7]

## Counterarguments

This paper does not argue that AI was irrelevant. Some reallocation toward AI was clearly real. The claim is narrower and stronger: AI became the legitimizing language layered onto a correction already driven by several deeper causes.[3][5]

## Limits of the Evidence

The paper is stronger on structure and narrative than on proving identical executive motives across firms. It shows a recurring pattern, not a single secret coordination mechanism.

## Reform Relevance

This chapter mainly supports the weights reform indirectly. It shows how a system built from collective human production can still be commercialized through narratives that weaken labor, narrow the protected tier, and normalize downward pressure on the technical middle class.[7]

## Conclusion

The layoff cycle was not born from one machine. It was narrated through one. That matters because the politics of enclosure depends not only on ownership of models, but on the social story used to justify who remains strategic and who becomes disposable.[3][7]
