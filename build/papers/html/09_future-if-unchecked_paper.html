Introduction

This paper asks: How does future if no action is taken function within the dossier’s larger argument, and what does the documentary record allow this chapter to establish? Its working answer is clear from the start: The most credible continuity scenario is not science-fiction apocalypse. It is a slower regime of deepening dependence, shrinking cognitive sovereignty, weakened middle-class bargaining power, and increasingly hierarchical access to machine intelligence. 2 The objective is not only to recount developments, but to show why the subject of this chapter belongs inside a structural argument about extraction, enclosure, legitimacy, and reform.

Historical or Institutional Context

The U.S. Department of Education has already framed AI in learning environments as a governance issue involving automated decisions, bias, privacy, and human oversight. Dependency concerns are therefore not merely cultural panic. 3

Main Analysis

The GPTs paper and the IMF note both support an uneven future in which exposure to generative AI is distributed asymmetrically across occupations, income positions, and labor systems. The future problem is not uniform extinction. It is differential vulnerability. 4

Compute-governance research supports the claim that high-end capability is likely to remain infrastructure-concentrated unless policy changes the terms of access. That means unequal access to machine intelligence is already an infrastructure question, not only a talent question. 5

The continuity scenario also includes unequal fallback. Some of the same actors asking institutions and publics to normalize deeper dependence on AI and platform infrastructure have also publicly described or been credibly reported as building private redundancy, jurisdictional exit, and survivability options: Altman’s 2016 prep disclosures and New Zealand fallback, Zuckerberg’s Kauai shelter complex, and Thiel’s long association with New Zealand retreat logic and elite-exit politics. That does not prove foreknowledge, coordinated collapse engineering, or a hidden command chain. It does show how continuity can stratify not only access to intelligence, but access to backup, redundancy, and escape when shared systems fail or become politically untrustworthy. 6

The financial and moral-warning layers also matter. The BIS warned of fragile capex dynamics and weak near-term returns in the AI buildout, while Dario Amodei publicly warned that AI-era wealth concentration could strain democratic society. These are not identical claims, but together they strengthen the dossier’s continuity scenario of hierarchy rather than broad emancipation. 7

Interpretation: if current platform habits, labor structures, weights enclosure, compute chokepoints, and asymmetric fallback systems continue reinforcing one another, the likely future is not total machine takeover but a regime of dependency, access hierarchy, and weaker cognitive sovereignty. A contextual formulation from Dion Wiggins is useful here: continuity can disguise dependency when systems still appear to function while effective authority migrates elsewhere. That is precisely why the dossier’s three reforms belong together rather than as isolated policy tweaks. 8
