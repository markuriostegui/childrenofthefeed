Introduction

This paper asks: How does imperial control over access function within the dossier’s larger argument, and what does the documentary record allow this chapter to establish? Its working answer is clear from the start: The AI system is not simply a market of competing products. It is also an architecture of chokepoints. Export controls, cloud dependence, chip access, and defense-state adjacency increasingly define who can build, scale, or even reach frontier intelligence. The question is no longer only who invents. It is who gets permission. 2 The objective is not only to recount developments, but to show why the subject of this chapter belongs inside a structural argument about extraction, enclosure, legitimacy, and reform.

Historical or Institutional Context

The United States has already moved from broad leadership rhetoric into formal doctrine, consultation processes, and implementation guidance. Executive action, federal requests for information, and corporate positioning now form a visible policy sequence rather than a vague background condition. 3

Main Analysis

BIS guidance gives the advanced-computing export regime an operational enforcement layer. The politics of AI access is therefore not merely metaphorical. It is already administrative, technical, and executable. 4

Hyperscaler dependence is also documented. At least one major frontier lab publicly tied the majority of its workloads and future development to a single cloud giant, which means frontier capability is already routed through a small number of infrastructural landlords. 5

The formal government-facing layer is now public as well. OpenAI’s OpenAI for Government, Anthropic’s Claude Gov, and the Palantir-AWS-Anthropic classified distribution lane show that frontier access is organized not only through markets but through accredited, restricted, and government-priority channels. 6

The personnel interface is explicit too. The Army’s Detachment 201 demonstrates that the boundary between senior frontier-tech leadership and military institutions has become porous in public view. That does not prove improper command. It does prove public institutional integration. 7

In-Q-Tel, Palantir, and the Thiel-linked network provide a serious documentary basis for talking about venture-state-security adjacency without drifting into mythology. The structure is the point: chips, clouds, accredited environments, contracts, and strategic interfaces remain highly concentrated even if some model knowledge diffuses. 8

Interpretation: this is where Limit Government Capture by AI Companies begins to look structurally necessary. Once public support, export-control protection, privileged state access, and defense channels become material to frontier advantage, the resulting intelligence cannot be defended as a purely ordinary private market outcome. 9

Counterarguments

This chapter must not confuse chokepoints with total closure. The evidence supports concentrated leverage, privileged lanes, and formal state-company interfaces, not omnipotent control over all capability diffusion. 10

Limits of the Evidence

This chapter must not confuse chokepoints with total closure. The evidence supports concentrated leverage, privileged lanes, and formal state-company interfaces, not omnipotent control over all capability diffusion. 11

Reform Relevance

If frontier AI is governed through chokepoints, then intelligence itself becomes a stratified geopolitical infrastructure. Access is no longer merely a subscription question. It becomes a question of empire, supply chains, protected lanes, and permission. 12

Conclusion

If frontier AI is governed through chokepoints, then intelligence itself becomes a stratified geopolitical infrastructure. But chokepoints alone do not explain public acceptance. In the architecture of this series, Chapter 07 therefore functions as a self-contained argument while also advancing the cumulative path toward the dossier’s three reforms.
